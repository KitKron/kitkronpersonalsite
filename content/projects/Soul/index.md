<title>A History of the Soul and its Impact on Brazil-United States Relations</title>

On March 13th, 2027, a group of military scientists at Bharathiar University in 
Coimbatore, India, discovered the existence of the soul. They were instructed to conceal their 
findings while continuing research into, what they labeled, “human essence”. This secret was 
held for three months and seventeen days, but soon the entire world knew the reality of the soul. 
A year later, in Basel, Switzerland, scientists working under Roche Molecular Diagnostics 
successfully achieved an electrical output from the soul of a brain-dead woman. They published 
their discovery, claiming that the total power extracted was equivalent to “the daily electrical 
consumption of 150 households”. However, in the process, the woman died. As more countries 
developed their own ways of collecting souls for energy, land disputes and small-scale conflicts 
in high population areas became increasingly common. World leaders focused on utilizing 
foreign souls to increase the quality of life within their nations. The first soul collection facility 
was opened on July 3rd, 2029, just outside Kano, Nigeria. The first “es-car” was created on 
October 27th, 2031, in San Diego. 
Spurred on by French and Italian territorial claims, conflict in the regions surrounding the 
Arabian sea began to increase throughout 2032. This led to a large-scale Euro-Asian unification 
movement, focused on stabilizing the global east. The campaign eventually spread across the 
entire northern hemisphere, accelerated by increased fear of global war. On June 14th, 2034, the 
“Essence Protection Treaty” (EsPT) was signed by 93 countries. Since then, conflict in the North 
has decreased exponentially. 
In the early months of 2041, Brazilian authorities made several announcements 
cementing their commitment to keep soul collection facilities nationalized. The recently elected 
president of the United States, Steven Price, subsequently called for a private meeting with his 
Secretary of Defense, Ryan Horne, to discuss U.S. influence in the region.
“This is an act of humiliation,” were the first words out of the President’s mouth. “They 
think they can make a mockery out of America.” The white overhead lights in the conference 
room were chosen to complement his skin, but the red that currently filled his cheeks made them 
worthless. Horne reclined in the seat next to him, leaving the other 12 chairs around the table 
empty, and jotted down his thoughts in a black binder marked with the seal of the U.S. army. 
“Mr. President, it is becoming increasingly clear that Brazil’s actions pose an existential threat to 
the United States,” he said slowly, his tone similar to one that would be used to ridicule a child. 
The President did not notice, the words appearing calculated and sharp in his mind. “Horne, I’m 
going to ask you a question, and it’s going to effect this entire thing, okay?” He smirked as he 
spoke, projecting his voice to regain control of the exchange. Horne nodded his head slowly as 
he locked eyes with the President, wrestling the rising anxiety in his chest, subduing it into a 
warped sense of confidence; something he had decades of practice doing. “Of course, Mr. 
President. Ask away.” The President smiled at him and raised his eyebrows, proud of Horne’s 
initiative. Through this uncomfortable combination of facial expressions, Horne got a good look 
at the effects of time on the man’s face; his usually baggy skin pulled taught by the tired muscles 
underneath. He thought for a moment about his own appearance at that age, but assured himself 
that he would be handsome until death, and maybe even after.
“How do you think this great nation can continue to not only survive, but be the best in 
the world, Horne? I know my answer,. I’ve given it a lot of thought.” The President continued to 
awkwardly smile at Horne, studying him while he considered the question. He thought about 
how Horne’s skin looked like it had been shrink-wrapped onto his skull, and how much he was 
balding at thirty-six. He thanked God that he stilled looked this handsome at sixty-eight. “As of 
right now, essence, Mr. President. The more we control, the taller we stand. Our great forefathers 
knew that coal and oil were the only way to take control of our destiny, and now we stand in the 
same position.” Horne congratulated himself on his answer with a smirk. The President’s grin 
shifted into a toothy smile, one that he had practiced tirelessly. “You’ve only got half of it Horne. 
Only half!” He said, followed by a raspy chuckle. Horne’s eyes widened slightly as the President 
spoke. “We need to assure the protection of the true patriots, those that deserve to live in these 
terrific states. We need to show that we cannot be shoved around and bullied. These guys down 
in Brazil, they need to be taught that you don’t mess with us, you don’t mess with America 
without repercussions.” 
The President wiped the film of sweat off his forehead with the cuff of his suit, staining it. 
Horne paused for a moment, letting the sound of the President’s slight wheezing fill the room. He 
glanced down at the notes he had taken earlier. “Make him agree” were the only words on the 
page. He turned back to the President, who gazed at him in a way that suggested his feelings 
were hurt. “You’re absolutely right, Mr. President. We need to make a show-of-force. The EsPT 
prevents us from direct action, of course, but we can work around that.” He lifted the top page in 
his binder, exposing a grocery list underneath. He pretended to read it, and paused for a moment 
to think. “If you give the order, we can do it, Steven. We can save and elevate our great nation’s 
reputation in one go, and with the added benefit of more access to essence farms.” He gave his 
best attempt at a smile of approval, weak as it was, to the President, who grinned widely back. 
The President shot out his hand, and was almost giddy when Horne shook it. “We’re doing God’s 
work, Horne.” Horne, hearing this, felt an almost renewed sense of patriotism. Maybe we really 
are, he thought.
On April 24th, 2041, U.S. operatives contacted Brazilian military official Antônio Luciano 
de Sousa, known as Tonho, and convinced him to initiate a U.S. funded coup d'état. A month 
later, 12 Brazilian officials had been killed, and Tonho was appointed president. U.S. 
involvement in the coup was not made public, but President Price stated that it was “a fantastic 
and much needed change for the country,” and hoped that the U.S. and Brazil could now “see 
eye-to-eye on issues of the modern day.” 
In June of the same year, Tonho attended several meetings with Price, all held at the 
White House. On July 3rd, the U.S. Press Secretary announced the formation of a new committee 
that would work with Tonho’s administration to “assist with the oversight and privatization of 
Brazil’s essence industry.” Tonho announced to news outlets that it was “a new and 
unprecedented era for the economic future of Brazil,” and Price called it “a big blow to socialism 
and a victory for democracy” on his social media accounts.
Large-scale protests erupted the next day throughout Brazil. The phrase “Uma Só Alma,” 
or “One Soul,” was used as a slogan, calling for the government to re-nationalize soul 
production. Brasília was used as a stage for the largest of the protests, with people bussing in 
from the surrounding areas in order to increase visibility. Tonho quickly deployed military police 
to try and quell the movement, calling it “an affront to the good people of Brazil.” This only 
served to escalate the conflict. A week later, a crowd of upwards of 10,000 people had gathered 
in Brasília. While there was no head orchestrator of the protest, several left-wing organizations 
based in and around the city made their presence known. One of these organizations, “União 
Revolucionária Futura,” or “Future Revolutionary Union,” consisting of a small band of people 
from the Cidade Ocidental region of Goiás, was one of the first groups to gather in front of the 
National Congress building on the morning of the protest.
Marcos tugged on his balaclava, pulling the thin fabric into a position that made breathing 
easier. The mask made him claustrophobic, and his double layer of sweatshirts, heavy cargo 
pants, and overstuffed backpack only intensified the feeling. His comrades had carefully planned 
the outfits a week prior, making sure they covered everything but the eyes and provided a good 
amount of padding. Everything they wore was black except for the grungy second-hand bike 
helmets that each of them had strapped to their backpacks, to be used when the police came. 
Their group, União Revolucionária Futura, consisted of eleven people, but only nine of them 
were present. Rato couldn’t take the time off work and Riven was taking care of his 
grandmother. Marcos tensed as he thought about them not being there to help. He looked out at 
the looming gray towers of the congressional building; the muted hues of dawn behind them 
frightened him.
“Curupira, how are feeling you right now?” Besouro, a member of the group that had 
only joined recently, walked over to him, positioning herself at his side. She had immigrated 
from the U.S. around a month ago, and was still working on her Portuguese. “I’m doing alright. 
A bit worried for later, I guess, when the cops show up.” When he talked his balaclava slipped 
into his mouth, soaking the loose fabric in saliva, making it hard to draw air. “Me too,” was all 
she said before turning her attention to the congressional building. She gripped a sign made from 
tattered cardboard that read “Souls are part of us. Humanity > Progress.” They sat in silence for a 
moment and stared at the building together; the slowly rising sun reflecting off the windows, 
hurting their eyes. She patted him on the back and then went to talk to someone else. Each time 
someone used his alias he would tense up and press his teeth together, even though he had picked 
it himself. He had gotten the name from an ugly cartoon painting of the Curupira on the outside 
of his pre-school. The painting had always scared him as a kid, and his dad would have to hold 
his hand whenever he went to school. A shiver went down his back, distracting him from the 
memory. He looked down at the grass in front of him, the well maintained lawn contrasting the 
gray street he stood on. A small piece of the asphalt had broken away and sunken into the grass. 
He picked it up, put it in his pocket, and went to join the members of the group in making signs, 
anxious for the crowds to form.
After a few hours, Marcos was face-to-face with a police officer. His group had created a 
bubble, with him and two comrades at the front and the others in the back holding signs, ready to 
support them or swap positions if anything went wrong. The groups surrounding them were in 
similar formations, with the bravest of them up against the armored police. They held signs and 
banners that read “One Soul,” or “Give Us Back Our Souls,” or some variant of the two. Marcos 
was forced into the officer in front of him by the weight of the crowd, the only separation 
between the two being a transparent riot shield. “You took everything from me! You took 
everything I had! I will make you pay!” he repeated, screaming at the officer at the top of his 
lungs as small bubbles of spit formed on the surface of his mask. Marcos felt absent in the 
moment; he focused on the eyes of the officer, hidden behind the glossy finish of the shield and a 
protective riot helmet. The man looked angry, the veins near his temples bulging from under his 
skin, pounding in rhythm with his heart. He didn’t know what the state had done. He didn’t know 
Marcos’ father, what a great man he was. He didn’t know what a cancer diagnosis meant in the 
country, how it’s easier to sell yourself than waste away. He didn’t know what it felt like to open 
a check for a sum that could barely pay for the funeral. Or maybe he did. Either way, in that 
moment, it was entirely his fault.
Marcos reached into his pocket, took out the piece of asphalt, and started to smash it 
against the officer’s head. Each time he forced the rock down, it would glide off the helmet, 
leaving a streak of ashy black. The officer, after a moment of shock, backed up while Marcos 
continued to hit him. Marcos knew what was coming, but the crowd behind him pushed against 
him fiercely, and his comrades weren’t there to help, as they were distracted by the other police 
in the line. Two officers filled in the other’s position, grabbed Marcos, and threw him towards 
them. He pounded at them desperately, the asphalt shedding pieces with each strike to their 
heads, until it crumbled in his hand.
Just as Marcos was about to cross beyond the line of police, he felt a force behind him, 
desperately pulling on his backpack in an attempt to wrench him back into the crowd. His 
balance faltered due to the polar forces, causing him to partially fall backwards, leaving his legs 
on the ground but his body held upright by the tugging of his backpack. He felt like a wounded 
soldier being carried across a battlefield. The police were now pulling on his legs, so he began to 
kick them, making sure to aim for their heads. He was finally able to conjure enough force to 
deliver a firm blow to one of the officers, causing him to fall backwards, the visor on his helmet 
breaking in the process. Marcos was dragged into the crowd as he whooped and yelled 
victoriously, his position quickly being replaced by one of his comrades. A clearing had been 
formed around him, allowing him to pick himself up. He brushed off the dirt and dust on his 
pants, feeling a rip in the fabric near his calf, and looked up expecting to see a member of his 
group. A woman without a mask, without black clothing or a helmet, stared back at him. “Are 
you okay?” she yelled, the noise of the crowd muffling her words. “I’m okay. Thank you. I 
would have been arrested if you hadn’t dragged me away,” Marcos shouted, baffled that the 
women did such a thing. She surveyed Marcos as if she was making sure that he was actually 
okay. “What’s your name?” she asked, moving closer to Marcos so he could hear her better. 
“Curupira.”
On the 16th of July, a CIA whistle blower sent documents to major news outlets detailing 
America’s involvement in the coup that placed Tonho in power. In response, “One Soul” protests 
erupted throughout the U.S. in solidarity with Brazil. These protests lasted until the 22nt of July, 
ending when Tonho announced a social program that would return a large percentage of the 
profits from private soul production to the people of Brazil. Six months later, Tonho quietly 
released a memo, stating that the social program would be placed in the control of the United 
States Brazilian Essence Security Committee. The day after, the CIA whistle blower was found 
dead in a hotel room in Columbus, Ohio, in an apparent suicide. 
Small groups, including those that were present at the larger “One Soul” protests, 
continue to grow in size due to this decision, but struggle to find influence over the Brazilian 
government. The “Future Revolutionary Union,” however, has been attempting to form a 
coalition between several of these organizations in an effort to gain wider political power. This 
has elevated their influence throughout the regions surrounding Brasília, but many collectives 
still refuse to join, questioning if “the union truly believes in the revolutionary politics, or if it’s 
just a part of their name.”
Yearly global soul production is expected to rise to 150 million by 2050, with Brazil 
contributing 20 million, or roughly 13%. Brazil and the U.S. remain close allies since the 
formation of Tonho’s government.
