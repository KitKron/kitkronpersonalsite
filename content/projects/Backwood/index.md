<title>Backwood Despondence</title>

The theater was small and cramped. There were only about twenty chairs in the room, and most of them were filled. Two small chandeliers hung low from the ceiling, giving me just enough light to navigate to the last available seat in the back row. The other people in the aisle squeezed against their chairs as I pushed by. It was my second or third time at The Higher Depths—I couldn’t recall if I had seen The American Clock here or at Carpenter Square a few seasons ago—but I had yet to be impressed with its productions. The theater’s insistence on hosting inexperienced writers and directors yielded plays that disrespected the medium as a whole. I had been writing theater reviews in the Oklahoma Times for about 10 years, and this show had been getting buzz in the local papers, so I was forced to attend against my better judgment. I held a notepad in my lap—I never understood how people could get through a play without a notepad, how else would one keep track of their thoughts?—and looked around at the other people in the audience. It seemed to be a split between droopy-eyed elders and blue-collar twenty-somethings. I sighed through my nose while scribbling down a note. Audience is who you’d expect.
	
Backwoods Despondence was the third play written and directed by Kate Cole, an indie darling in the Oklahoma theater scene. From what I had gathered from her interviews in the Oklahoma City Tribune, she had grown up just outside of Ada and was able to become a career playwright through a Chickasaw Nation-sponsored scholarship. It wasn’t that I was skeptical of her ability to make a good play, but the productions I had seen from newer writers who grew up in the country were hard to classify as art. They were usually lackluster retellings of something Tennessee Williams wrote fifty years ago, and I wasn’t interested in watching actors mull on about how dire everything was in the South and how it was all the government’s or corporations’ fault. I knew otherwise, as five years ago Integrated Communications Limited—a conglomerate that owned most of the major news outlets in Oklahoma, Arkansas, and Louisiana—bought the Oklahoma Times. Before then the Times was struggling to give me a proper paycheck, but after the acquisition, they moved our office into the old civics center in downtown Oklahoma City, and expanded it into a state-wide newspaper. The whole thing gave me an even wider audience for my reviews, so I owe my career to them.

The chandeliers dimmed as the faded red curtain that obscured the stage was pulled back. The darkness was quickly replaced with bright yellow-tinted spotlights that directed my attention to center-stage where a bar counter—made of plywood and painted a dark brown—sat. An older man behind the counter faced the audience as he pretended to clean a beer glass with a rag. He wore a plain brown t-shirt and had another rag slung over his shoulder. A small neon sign hung from the ceiling behind the actor that read “C.C.C. Ale” and three tattered stools rested in front of the bar. I looked down at my notepad and used the light that bounced off the stage to scribble another comment. Substandard set design. Another older man hobbled into the scene from off-stage, grunting as he sat down on one of the bar stools. He wore a ragged army-green coat. He was facing away from the audience and towards the man who played the bartender, a decision that I found irritating. How was I supposed to get to know the characters if I couldn’t see them? I shifted uncomfortably in my seat, it’s old hinges squeaking loudly.

“Hey there Frank,” the bartender said to the old man awkwardly, placing the beer glass behind the flimsy counter.
“Evenin’ Joseph,” the old man responded. I strained to hear his muffled voice as it echoed off the back of the stage. No consideration for audience. “How’s the day treatin’ ya.”
“Oh, you know. Not many faces tonight. Trying to take to it on the chin.” The bartender reached under the counter and pulled out an already-prepared glass of brown dyed water in a crystal whiskey glass.
“You see they finally demolished the ol’ school up there at the edge of town? Watched ‘em knock it down with one of ‘em excavators. Sad sight to see, used to go there when I was a youngin.” I sighed loudly—the woman on my left glaring at me as I did—and took another note. Actors passable at best.
“Damn. Never went there myself. Knew it meant a lot to people though.” The bartender went back to cleaning the same beer glass from before.
“Signs said they were buildin’ a new factory for Consolidated Connections, somethin’ to do with makin’ electronics. Said somethin’ about a distillery too. Not sure what they got to do with each other.” The old man took a big swig of the fake whiskey. I glanced at the other people in my aisle. They seemed curious, like they couldn’t tell where the story was headed. I was right about what I was in for. These kinds of productions can never live up to the praise.
“Can’t say I’m surprised. Seems like they’ve been eyeing that land for a while now. Hope it at least makes shipping those cheaper.” The bartender gestured with his head up to the neon sign. The white light pulsing through the letters contrasted the dark bar counter. Symbolism lacks subtlety.
“That stuff’s sewage water.” The old man said, taking another drink of his whiskey prop. The audience laughed. I winced in embarrassment. “That company can’t make anythin’ good. I bought one of their telephones with last month’s pension’n the damn thing don’t even ring half the time. We were fine before they swooped in’n started makin’ every godforsaken thing under the sun.” The old man slammed a fist down on the counter, the plywood creaking under the force, the noise reaching my ears better than his voice. I was already sick of the play. Why would anyone care about an old school getting repurposed into something truly productive? I was baffled that Cole was trying to advocate against something that would actually help such a rural community. It was insulting. Cole’s writing is under-baked. 
“Calm down Frank. It ain’t all bad. Least folks might get some new jobs.” That was the first line that actually made sense to me, but I knew that the bartender would be proven wrong somehow. There was no room for level-headed characters in this play. The bartender continued to clean the beer glass. The old man scoffed and finished off his drink.

Two more actors entered from off-stage—a woman in her mid-20s and a man in his early 30s—and sat down on the remaining two stools in front of the bar, facing away from the audience like the older man. The younger woman immediately enamored me. She was dressed in a white overcoat with a lime-green undershirt and black jeans. She wore black ropers that clicked against the stage when she walked. Her straight black hair reached half-way down her back, the spotlights giving it a stunning shine. I wished she was facing me.

“Hey Joseph,” the woman said. Her voice reached me easily. I relaxed back into my seat, glad there was finally a reason to pay attention. “How’s business.”
“Hey there Eula. Trying not to complain. Gets harder every day,” the bartender said flatly. He reached under the counter and pulled out a filled wine glass and slid it over to her. “Don’t worry. It’s the cheap stuff. You two come together?”
“Me and David? Na we pulled into the parking lot at the same time. I came from my parents’.” Eula looked over to the man she had entered the stage with. He was wearing a navy-blue canvas jacket and brown work boots. The bartender pretended to survey the worker for a moment, then reached under the counter and slid him an empty beer bottle with a white label. I couldn’t make out the letters on it from where I was, but it was clear it was the “C.C.C. Ale” that the neon sign was advertising. I put a large exclamation mark next to my note about the play’s symbolism.
“I came from work, was helpin’ knock down that old school—y’all know that one? Up by that Calvary church?” The worker pretended to take a big swig from the empty bottle. I looked over my notes while he recited his lines. I didn’t care what he had to say, I just wanted Eula to keep talking.
“Frank was just talking about that. Says that they’re building a Consolidated Connections facility there. That true?” The bartender said as he slipped the old man another cup of “whiskey”. My eyes focused on Eula as she sipped on her wine. Cole wastes time on unimportant information.
“Yeah, company finally managed to buy the land—thank God they did, finally‘ll have a steady paycheck buildn’ that thing. Wife’ll be happy about it too.” I furrowed my brow as the worker said the line. Why would Cole reduce what the company could do for them down to a simple paycheck? Integrated Communications was working to unify and inform people, not just give them salary and send them home. It was belittling to say otherwise. My pay didn’t increase after they acquired the Times, but that was because they put that money towards making the paper better for the entire state. I waited impatiently for Eula to speak again, hoping that she would have a more measured outlook.
“We would’ve been fine without it,” the old man grumbled. I was starting to get a headache from trying to pick up the actors’ voices. I massaged my neck—letting out an exasperated grunt—and the woman next to me shot me another look. “Plenty of work here already, just nobody wants to do it.”
“C’mon Frank. You don’t think what they’re doing is good for us? Seems like a good way out of the pit this town’s in. Hell the only reason I went to college was because of the scholarship they gave me through the Nation.” I was relieved to hear Eula speak, and ecstatic that she actually knew what she was talking about. Her voice held power. Even the actors stopped for a moment to consider her words. A breakout performance by Eula.
“We were gettin’ along fine before they started takin’ over the whole damn town. Hard work’n cash’s all they want, and the Lord knows they’ll suck us dry then leave us hangin’.” I was excited to hear Eula’s response. Perhaps I was too quick to judge and Cole actually did know what she was doing, despite her upbringing. I put a large question mark next to the notes I had already written.
“Maybe you and your people were Frank, but you know mine weren’t. We barely had running water ten years ago, but we adapted because we had to. We built our own companies so we could help each other, and I don’t see much difference between that and what Consolidated Connections is doing. It’s the only way to move the community forward in the world you’ve built around us.” The old man sat silently, seemingly overpowered by Eula’s words. I sat upright with my eyes wide, her words echoing in my mind. Her community used companies to assist and connect each other, much like the Times and Integrated Communications did. She was able to rise above hardship because of them. Cole truly understood what most people in her position didn’t: companies are the ways we progress. Cole courageously captures modern issues.
“Seems like you got it all figured out,” the old man growled, finishing off his second prop whiskey. He stood up from the counter and stumbled his way off the stage, disappearing behind the folded red curtain. Good riddance.
“Come on Eula. Give the old man a break. He’s just nostalgic, can’t make sense of the town changing,” the bartender said plainly. I was shocked to hear him say such a thing. What right did he have to oppose Eula? She knew what was best for them.

Eula stood up and barged off the stage, her boots clicking with each step. The bartender and the worker followed her with their heads until she disappeared, then looked at each other for a moment. I was saddened to see her leave. The worker feigned finishing off his beer and slid the bottle towards the bartender. The bartender quietly slid him another empty bottle with the same white label, then went back to cleaning the beer glass. The spotlights dimmed slowly, leaving the theater in complete darkness. The audience whispered to themselves about the excellent acting or the interesting story, but all I could think about was how wrong I was about how fantastic Eula was, and how wrong I was about Cole. I was overjoyed to see an artist who truly understood what we as a community needed. Sensational.

The spotlights switched back on, flooding the stage with light again. The bar counter was the same as before, and the bartender was still behind it cleaning the beer glass. He wore a plain white t-shirt now. The neon sign flashed between white and blue, which I found distracting. The worker slumped in from off-stage, crashing onto one of the bar stools, it’s metal base banging against the floor. He rested his head in his arms on the counter. He wore a white shirt with small holes in it. I shook my head slightly. That’s what clamoring for a paycheck gets you.

“You alright David? Haven’t seen you for a while.” the bartender said awkwardly. He slid the worker an empty white-labeled beer bottle from under the bar. The worker raised his head from his arms, looked at the bottle, then dropped it back down. “Don’t make a fuss. You know the stuff’s cheap.”
“You’re lucky Joseph. Can’t find no damn work in this town. No jobs since that facility—the one up by that church, not the one near the supermarket—went’n got built. Least you still got the bar.” The worker made a show of taking a big swig from the beer bottle, slamming it down on the counter after. The sound echoed loudly off the stage. I slouched in my seat and tapped my pen against my notepad. The woman next to whispered something in her husband’s ear and gave me another look. I just wanted Eula to come back.
“Yeah, well. Can’t say I’m doing so hot either. Had to make a deal with that damn company. Only way to keep the place alive.” The bartender took out another beer bottle and pretended to take a sip, placing it on the counter afterward. I didn’t understand how receiving help through the company was harmful, especially if it was the only way for him to keep the bar open. Seemed like a good deal to me. I bet Eula would have some choice words for the man.
“Hard times these days. Been tryin’ to get the wife to take me back in, but she won’t have none of it. Hell’m I supposed to do. There’s just no damn work.” The worker tried to weep, but it sounded more like he was yelling in pain. I rolled my eyes and sunk further into my seat. Actors cannot accommodate Cole’s effective writing.

The old man shuffled in from off-stage and sat next to the worker, seemingly refusing to acknowledge him. He wore a plain white t-shirt—similar to the bartender’s. The bartender looked at the sobbing worker for a moment, then reached under the counter and slid the old man a beer. When would she come out?

“Hell’s this? Where’s my whiskey?” the old man yelled, his voice ricocheting loudly off the back of the stage. The echo was starting to give me a headache. The worker stopped crying and laid his head back on the counter.
“Consolidated Connections Corporation Ale. It’s all I got now, Frank. Leave if you don’t like it. Settle up first if you do. Can’t afford to keep putting drinks on your tab,” the bartender said sternly. The old man grumbled and took a swig of the beer. I didn’t understand why Cole would be wasting so much time on characters that failed to see the good that was coming to them. Their reluctance to adapt to change was upsetting enough. Focusing more on Eula would have been better. Cole falters at points, but it doesn’t diminish her talent.
“Same damn company that’s makin’ this beer made me lose my pension Joseph, you know that. Just down to Social Security now’n three-hundred dollars ain’t cuttin’ it. Had to beg the church to help with groceries. I’ll settle when I can.” The bartender looked at the old man intensely, but didn’t respond. The three men sat for a moment drinking their prop beers.

Finally, Eula came in from off-stage. She made her way to the bar counter and rested on a stool as the bartender slid her a beer. Her entire outfit—overcoat, undershirt, pants, and boots—was now white. I sat up in my seat, ecstatic that she was finally on stage. She would show these people what good can come from such circumstances. I awaited her voice eagerly. 

“C’mon Joseph. You know I hate this stuff,” Eula said. Her voice sounded deflated, and I was having trouble hearing her. Where had her confidence gone? I frowned in confusion and fidgeted with my pen. “Stop joking and get me the usual.”
“He don’t got anythin’ else, just this damn backwash. Said he made a deal or somethin’,” the old man responded angrily, raising his bottle to his mouth. Eula looked at him for a moment then took a sip of the beer. I was worried that Cole had tricked me. Maybe she didn’t know what she was doing, why else would Eula be so dejected? Does Cole really understand?
“What’s his deal,” Eula said, gesturing to the worker, his head still buried in his arms. The old man shrugged as the bartender passed him another empty bottle.
“He’s having trouble finding work. Not much to go around since that facility was built,” the bartender responded coldly.
“Seems everyone’s struggling. Nation hasn’t been doing so great either. Consolidated Connections keeps buying up our businesses. Most folks can’t afford to say no. Governor’s thinking of selling the land the community center’s on to them. Only way to keep the tribal clinic open.” Eula spoke somberly, periodically stopping to take a large swig of her beer. I didn’t understand, she was supposed to be the level-headed one. She was supposed to seize the great opportunities that were before her. Why was she doing this? “We were just trying to get ahead.”
“Y’know Eula, I’ve been thinkin’ about that fight we had a while back. I think I understand what you were sayin’, not much to be done these days to try’n get a leg up but play by the rules. Can’t say I think it was a good idea, but I understand,” the old man said, turning his head to Eula. I sat upright at the edge of my seat so I could clearly hear Eula’s response. It would either cement Cole as a great playwright, or make her nothing better than the thousands of other people who couldn’t grasp what true progress was.
“No Frank. You don’t understand. You never could,” Eula said sharply. The tension I was holding loosened a bit. Maybe she could redeem herself. “But I’m glad that you tried. And that you actually listened to me for once. We’re not in the same position, but we can still help each other. I wanted you to realize that.” I couldn’t believe it. I took my pen and fiercely scratched out everything I had written. Eula—no, Cole—didn’t know what she was doing. She didn’t know what helping a community looked like. There was no future in the way she wrote. She was just another lazy playwright who imitated those before her. Cole is just another amateur who doesn’t understand the form.

The actors sat silently for a moment, as if Cole wanted the audience to really contemplate her words. I sunk back into my seat and sighed. The woman next to me whispered to her husband again. If only she knew how bad this play was, she would be thanking me for distracting her so much. I looked at my page of notes—half of them scratched out—and wondered why Cole would squander such potential. Maybe if she knew me and how Integrated Communications had saved me, she would understand. She just doesn’t get it.

“I’ll have your back Eula. Same goes for you two,” the bartender said earnestly, pointing at the worker—who had raised his head after Eula’s outburst—and the old man. I really didn’t care what any of the actors had to say anymore. I stared blankly at the stage, my head throbbing from trying to listen and understand them for so long.
“Given’ me a lot to think about Eula, but I want’cha to know that I want to help if I can. Don’t have much in the way of cash now, but we can figure somethin’ out,” the old man said. I fidgeted with my pen and pressed on my temples. I just wanted it to be over. “Same deal for you David. Joseph, I’ll only help if you start servin’ my whiskey again.”
“Not much I can do for y’all right now—gotta get back on my feet before then—but I’ll do the same,” the worker said, his words barely reaching me from the echo of the stage. I looked upwards at the chandeliers and started to count the rhinestones hanging from them.
“Good to hear some spirit from y’all,” Eula said as she pretended to finish off her beer. “Also David that’s nonsense, you don’t have to do that alone. We’ll help where we can. And Joseph, I’m with Frank on this, we have to get you serving my wine again.”

The four raised their prop bottles to each other and acted out a drink together. The spotlights dimmed, and after a moment the audience erupted in applause. The people around me stood up and clapped—some even cheered—while I sat slouched in my seat. The idea of so much praise towards such a production infuriated me. Cole didn’t deserve this. The applause died down as the spotlights switched back on. The actors lined up in front of the bar counter and took a bow. It was odd to see them all face towards the audience. They all looked so proud of themselves. Eula’s actress was front-and-center, and I was finally able to get a clear look at her. She was underwhelming, her face like any other’s. I couldn’t believe that I had been so enamored with her. I stood up, pushing past the other people in the aisle, and left the theater.

The next day I wrote my review on Backwoods Despondence, giving it a single star out of five. I touched on Kate Cole’s lackluster writing and directing, the actors’ poor performances, and the insulting view of progress in Oklahoma. My review was published in the Sunday paper. A year later, Cole received an award of excellence from the Oklahoma Community Theater Association. She is now a well known playwright, undeserving as she is of the title. I kept writing reviews for the times, but as the years went on they kept shortening my column little by little. Last month I was finally laid off. Integrated Connections needed to do some restructuring, and I was deemed non-essential. I was disappointed to lose my audience, but I’m still forever grateful that they allowed me such an opportunity.
